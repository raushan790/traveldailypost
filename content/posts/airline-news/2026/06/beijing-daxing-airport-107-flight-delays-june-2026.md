What makes today's situation particularly troubling is the ratio of delays to cancellations. For every cancelled flight, roughly 12 others are running late. That's not random congestion—that's a systemic operational breakdown.

Today, carriers chose option two almost universally, suggesting they're banking on the ability to recover operations later. That's optimistic thinking. History suggests otherwise.

## Why Is This Happening?

The official word from the airport and airlines remains vague, but aviation disruptions rarely stem from a single cause. [According to aviation operations experts, the most common culprits include](https://www.icao.int/safety/Pages/default.aspx):

**Adverse weather** ranks high. Thunderstorms, heavy rainfall, or wind shear over Beijing can temporarily choke airport capacity, forcing authorities to space aircraft further apart, reducing throughput dramatically.

**Air traffic management interventions** are another likely factor. When an airport approaches saturation, authorities deliberately slow aircraft movements to maintain safe separation—it's the right decision operationally but brutal for schedules.

**Aircraft rotation delays** cascade quickly. One late arrival throws off all subsequent departures. By mid-morning, you're looking at a domino effect across dozens of flights.

**Crew duty-time constraints** also play a role. When delays push crews toward regulated maximum duty hours, airlines must either cancel flights or risk regulatory violations.

Ground handling bottlenecks—baggage, refueling, catering—can also add up quickly during peak operational periods.
