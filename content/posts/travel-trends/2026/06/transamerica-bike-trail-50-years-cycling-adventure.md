In the summer of 1976, something extraordinary happened on the backroads of America: **4,000 cyclists** pedaled out of **Yorktown, Virginia**, bound for the Pacific Ocean. Most rode Schwinn bikes in short-shorts. None had GPS. All of them were chasing a dream born from a typewriter in Mexico and the vision of four adventurers who believed bikes could reveal the soul of a nation.

Fifty years later, the **TransAmerica Bicycle Trail** is still drawing riders every summer—and it's more relevant than ever.

## The Trail That Changed Everything

The origin story is pure Americana: In 1973, two couples—**Dan and Lys Burden** from Ohio, and **Greg and June Siple**—were biking from Alaska to Argentina on what they called the Hemistour. Traveling by bike forced them to slow down, to notice things, to connect with the places they passed through in a way that no car could ever allow.

That intimacy sparked an idea. Why not organize a massive cross-country bike ride to celebrate America's **Bicentennial in 1976**? The timing was perfect. The mission was pure: to show riders "what our country is all about, what it is and who it is."

Working with **National Geographic's first female senior editor, Carolyn Patterson**, the group found funding. Lys Burden, a trained geologist, drove the entire country handpicking small towns, backroads, and scenic geological regions. They mailed flyers to every adult bike rider they knew. International riders signed up from the **Netherlands, Japan**, and beyond.

By summer 1976, the trail was alive with cyclists—solo riders, small groups, all relaying their whereabouts to family via scattered phonebooths across the landscape.

## What Makes This Trail Different?

This isn't just a route. It's a curated masterpiece of **50 summers of tradition**.

**Mike Bowen**, who completed the trail in 2024, put it perfectly: "There's a comfort level in knowing it's already there. Plug and play." The route includes specific directions—even which police station to visit for permission to camp in town parks. An invisible network of hospitality emerges along the way.

Old ladies tending dahlias invite cyclists to church picnics. In **Afton, Virginia**, **June Curry** has been handing out homemade cookies to riders every summer since 1976. A gas station cashier in Kentucky refills your water bottle and recommends the best biscuits and gravy in the state—his cousin has been making them for 30 years.

The trail is called "the **Appalachian Trail** for biking," and for good reason. Generations of American families have embraced riders from around the world, inviting them to sleep in backyards and town parks. After a 70-mile day, swimming in a town pool feels like winning the jackpot.

The soul of this trail isn't in the mountain passes or national parks—though those are stunning. It's in the small-town gas stations of Kentucky, the country roads and family businesses that collectively tell America's story. It's in the **Colorado Rockies**, the **Mississippi River**, the **Ozarks of Missouri**, and the flat farmland of Kansas where water towers mark the towns.

The route climbs through the **Blue Ridge Mountains** and the steep **Appalachians**, crosses Huckleberry Finn country, and turns north at **Pueblo, Colorado**. Riders acclimate before tackling the highest passes, celebrate the Fourth of July in **Yellowstone National Park**, marvel at the **Grand Tetons**, and finish with the otherworldly lava fields at **McKenzie Pass** before reaching the Pacific.

## A Summer Journey West: One Rider's Story

In May 2004, a recent college graduate and her cycling partner loaded sleeping bags and a tent onto their racks and pedaled west from Virginia against prevailing winds. They relied on 12 folded paper maps and a guidebook listing trail turns, free camping spots, and town pools. To save weight, they ripped out pages along the way.

Starting from **Charlottesville, Virginia** near her alma mater, the journey unfolded like a film reel:

**Kentucky** meant perfecting tuna-and-ramen camping dinners and discovering regional biscuits and gravy. Two young men joined forces along the way, and the group became inseparable through **Missouri's Ozarks** and the flat Kansas farmland where 70-mile days were routine.

**Colorado** brought the Rockies. The climb over **Hoosier Pass** required acclimatization. In **Yellowstone**, they bear-bagged food at night and watched Old Faithful explode in the golden light. The peaks of the **Grand Tetons** mirrored in pristine lakes left them speechless.

**Montana** meant a pilgrimage to **Adventure Cycling** in **Missoula**—the founding organization itself—where afternoon tubing down the **Clark Fork River** felt like a blessing. The **Salmon River Canyon** unfolded in curves and shadows. **Oregon's McKenzie Pass** delivered otherworldly lava fields. And finally, after three months and countless sunrises, the Pacific Ocean.

Every day brought riders farther west than they'd ever traveled. The trail became more than the sum of its parts—it became a transformation.

## How to Ride the TransAmerica Trail Today

## When to Go

Summer is essential. Mountain passes aren't bikeable in winter. While cyclists have traveled both directions since 1976, the traditional route runs east to west against prevailing westerlies. Trail wisdom: get a pre-dawn start in flat states like Kansas to beat the winds.

## What You'll Need

For self-supported bikepackers camping and cooking, choose a hybrid touring bike with panniers. Essential gear includes a lightweight sleeping bag, tent, and camping stove for evening meals and morning coffee. Padded bike shorts are non-negotiable.

To travel lighter, stay in hostels and hotels instead of camping, and eat in restaurants and cafes. Every pound counts on a 4,250-mile journey.

The **Adventure Cycling Association**—the legacy organization of the original Bikecentennial—sells detailed **TransAmerica Trail paper maps** and an app version, complete with low-traffic road routes, elevation profiles, and essential services. [Visit Adventure Cycling's official site](https://www.adventurecycling.org/) for detailed route planning.

Modern cyclists also benefit from resources like [Warmshowers](https://warmshowers.org/), a global network of bike-touring hospitality, and cycle-touring communities on [Reddit's r/bicycletouring](https://www.reddit.com/r/bicycletouring/) that share current trail conditions and recommendations.

## The Legacy Continues

Fifty years after 4,000 cyclists pedaled into American history, the TransAmerica Trail remains what it was always meant to be: a gateway to understanding your country from a bicycle seat. The small towns still welcome riders. The old ladies still tend their dahlias. The invisible network of hospitality still works its magic on skeptics.

This summer, thousands will pedal from Virginia toward Oregon, carrying only what fits in their panniers and a heart full of curiosity. They'll discover what the Burdens and Siples discovered in 1973—that the real America isn't on a highway or in a guidebook. It's on the quiet backroads, in the faces of strangers who become friends, and in the quiet moment when you reach the ocean and realize you've pedaled yourself into a new understanding of home.

*The TransAmerica Trail doesn't just teach you where America is—it teaches you who America is.*
