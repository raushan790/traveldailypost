## From Exile to Imprisonment: How an Island Lost Its People

The February sun beats down on **Cala Reale** as I stand before the twin monuments that define **Asinara Island's** haunting modern history: the elegant **Palazzo Cala Reale**, once a royal retreat, and the crumbling **Stazione Sanitaria Marittima**, a sanitary checkpoint frozen in time. But the real story begins not with what was built here, but with what was taken away.

In 1885, the Italian government made a fateful decision. Intensifying maritime trade in the Mediterranean demanded an isolated quarantine station to prevent the spread of infectious diseases from arriving ships. The solution? Evict the island's entire population. Approximately 500 people of Sardinian and Ligurian origins—families with generations of roots—were forcibly removed from their homes. A new village, **Stintino**, was hastily created on the Sardinian mainland to house the exiles. "It was an exodus in the true sense of the word," explains **Alessandro Masala**, a freediver whose grandparents were among those displaced. "By the time the national park was established, the asinaresi (Asinara people) no longer existed."

## The Island That Swallowed 7,000 Souls

When World War I erupted, **Asinara** transformed from quarantine station into a prison camp. By 1915, the island was drowning in catastrophe. The Italian government transported 24,000 **Austro-Hungarian war prisoners** across the Balkans to this remote speck of Mediterranean rock. The island was utterly unprepared.

Within months, a devastating **cholera outbreak** ravaged the camp. Nearly 7,000 prisoners—roughly one-third of all arrivals—died in agony. Their bodies piled up faster than burial crews could dig graves. The chapel built by these desperate prisoners still stands today, its stained glass windows painted by Hungarian artist **István Szász** serving as a haunting memorial. Just over a mile away, the monumental cross-shaped **Austro-Hungarian Ossuary**, constructed in 1936, holds the remains extracted from mass graves.

## The Fascist Terror Years

But **Asinara's** darkness had only begun to deepen. In 1937, Italian colonial forces faced an assassination attempt in Ethiopia. When **Viceroy Rodolfo Graziani** was targeted by Ethiopian resistance fighters, **Benito Mussolini** ordered immediate reprisal—the infamous **Addis Ababa massacre** that killed thousands of innocent monks and civilians.

Among those spared but exiled to **Asinara** were 300 members of the Ethiopian elite. One was **Princess Romanework**, eldest daughter of **Emperor Haile Selassie**, who endured the death of her two-year-old son, **Gideon**, in Cala Reale's isolation.

## The Mafia Island: Article 41-bis Maximum Security

After World War II, planners dreamed of converting **Asinara** into a nature preserve. That vision would wait another 50 years.

Starting in 1971, the Italian state revived the island's isolation strategy—this time to contain the spreading influence of the **Mafia** and, later, leaders of the **Red Brigades**, a far-left militant organization active from 1970 to 1988. The extreme isolation ironically allowed criminal networks to maintain external communications while cutting off the outside world.

The prison's final and most infamous chapter began in 1985 when judges **Giovanni Falcone** and **Paolo Borsellino** drafted the **Maxiprocesso**—the largest organized crime trial in Italian history. Both judges, assassinated in 1992 for their crusade against the Mafia, turned **Asinara** into the epicenter of the **Article 41-bis "hard prison" system**, a regime of total isolation designed specifically for mafia bosses like **Salvatore Riina**.

## Escape and Oblivion

The prison's security was nearly absolute. Only one inmate in the entire history of **Asinara's** maximum-security operations managed to escape: **Matteo Boe**, a Sardinian prisoner who, in 1986, jumped aboard a speedboat piloted by accomplices to freedom. The odds were astronomical; few dared attempt what Boe achieved.

By 1997, the gates shut for the final time. The Italian state abandoned its island fortress.

## When Nature Reclaimed Everything

What happened next was unexpected. The very isolation that made **Asinara** a perfect prison created something far more valuable: an accidentally pristine Mediterranean sanctuary.

For over a century, the island had been sealed off from mass tourism, industrial fishing, and human development. Marine life exploded in the absence of human predation. The restricted access inadvertently preserved one of the most biodiverse marine ecosystems in the entire Mediterranean.

In 1997, **Asinara National Park** was officially established. Today, the island hosts something almost surreal: free-roaming herds of **Asinara donkeys**—rare albino donkeys indigenous to the island and facing extinction—now hopping through the ruins of the prison complex beside juniper bushes and wild horses.

## Diving Into History Underwater

When freediver **Alessandro Masala** began exploring **Asinara's** 68-mile coastline in the early 2000s, he discovered something extraordinary. "It was immediately clear to me that there's no place in Sardinia like it—in fact, only a few spots in the whole Mediterranean can claim such a level of environmental integrity," he recalls.

At just 10 meters depth, Masala encounters barracudas, sea breams, groupers, and seabeds of stunning flora. But his most remarkable discovery came in 2023: the **Sogliola**, a shipwreck lost for 80 years.

The **Sogliola** had served the **Italian Royal Navy** during World War II, transporting military vehicles. In 1943, **British submarines** intercepted and sank the vessel. "The shipwreck shows exactly how nature has reclaimed its space," Masala explains. "Untouched by humans, the wreck has acted as a magnet, evolving until it has become something entirely alive."

The island demands respect. Former prison cells with rusting bars still stand. The chapel built by dying prisoners remains. The ossuary holds the dead. Yet above it all, donkeys graze where guards once patrolled, and pristine waters hide secrets that have waited a century to be rediscovered.

**Asinara** is no longer a place of exile or imprisonment. It is something far more powerful: proof that nature, given time and isolation, can erase even the darkest human chapters and begin again.

*Once a fortress of human suffering, Asinara Island now whispers a different story—one of resilience, restoration, and the boundless power of nature to heal.*
