The weather was turning ugly over the Mackenzie Basin, and I had maybe four hours to find a bird that fewer than 200 of its kind had survived to see another breeding season. The **black stilt**—or *kakī* in Māori—is quite possibly Earth's most fragile wading bird, clinging to existence in one of New Zealand's most spectacular and remote landscapes. I was racing a storm system to join the hunt.

## Racing Against Weather and Extinction

Clouds raced across the **46,950-acre Glenmore Station**, a sprawling merino sheep, deer, and cattle estate anchored in the Southern Alps. Just hours earlier, the same weather front had grounded my helicopter en route to **The Bad Decision**, the country's highest whisky bar perched above the station. Now, instead of sipping mountain drams, I was bouncing across rough high-country tracks in a four-wheel drive with **Ben Laffan**, co-founder of **Tekapo Adventures**, searching for something exponentially rarer than rare spirits.

The Mackenzie Basin has its own microclimatic personality. Ringed by mountain ranges on all sides, it usually shelters from incoming weather systems. Not today. In four hours, rain would envelope the entire basin and extinguish even the stars visible in the **Aoraki Mackenzie International Dark Sky Reserve** for two full nights.

"Their presence tells us the ecosystem is functioning," Ben explained, eyes scanning the braided riverbeds stretching toward the Southern Alps. "If the kakī disappeared, it would signal a decline in the health of the entire river system."

## The World's Most Vulnerable Wader

What makes the black stilt so spectacularly rare? Evolution played a cruel joke on them.

**Fewer than 200 adult black stilts remain in the wild**—almost all confined to this single region on New Zealand's South Island. They're smaller than expected, resembling delicate oyster catchers with stark black plumage and needle-thin, pinkish-red legs. They step daintily through shallow waters and backwaters, probing for invertebrates with precision-engineered bills.

But here's the heartbreaking part: they evolved without natural predators. When introduced **stoats, ferrets, and feral cats** arrived with European settlers, the kakī had no fight-or-flight instinct. They didn't know how to flee. They couldn't survive the ambush.

"They didn't evolve with predators," Ben said matter-of-factly as we crouched in the long grass. "They don't exactly have a fight-or-flight response."

## Conservation on the Ground

The **New Zealand Department of Conservation** has ramped up intensive management. During spring breeding season (September through November in the Southern Hemisphere), conservation teams monitor nesting pairs obsessively. They remove eggs to raise chicks in captive breeding programs, then release juveniles once they're strong enough to survive in the wild.

This hands-on approach is working—slowly. But it requires vigilance, funding, and access to the remote stations where these birds live.

## The New Tekapo Adventures Experience

Ben and his wife Cristina founded Tekapo Adventures after years of guiding in the region. They noticed something obvious: guests were regularly encountering black stilts and other native birds during their tours. Why not make that the entire point?

"We were regularly encountering kākī and other native birds while guiding here, and over time it became clear the birdlife should be the central focus, not just something you happen to see," Ben explained. "We launched a dedicated birding tour this year, to give guests time to observe ethically and understand the ecology behind what they're seeing."

The **Black Stilt Birding Tour** is the result—a newly launched experience that takes travelers deep into private high-country stations across the Mackenzie Basin. Ben has spent years building trust within farming communities and securing access to land once entirely closed to the public.

## A Changing Landscape: Farming Meets Tourism

The economics of South Island sheep and beef farming are brutal. Rising costs for fuel, fertiliser, and land management have forced many stations to diversify. **Agritourism is reshaping the high country**—farms are blending conservation work with small-scale visitor access and guided experiences.

**Braemar Station** near Lake Pukaki now offers luxury cottages and lodges. **Glenmore Station** is just beginning its tourism transition, with tightly managed access and marked tracks protecting the fragile ecosystem.

This pivot opens wilderness formerly sealed from public access. For birders hunting the kakī, it's a game-changer.

## Spring Brings the Show

May isn't ideal timing for black stilt spotting—but spring (September through November) transforms the basin into a birdwatching spectacle.

"The braided rivers fill with nesting and feeding species," Ben said, driving along wetland edges where sightings concentrate. "You'll see **black-fronted terns** overhead, **dotterels** along the gravel banks, and **wrybills**—the world's only bird with a sideways-curving bill."

On this May afternoon, we tracked **Kārearea** (New Zealand falcons) soaring overhead and heard the harsh honking of **paradise ducks**—birds that, Ben noted, mate for life, just like the kakī.

## Into the Cass Valley

We descended into the **Cass River**, where pale channels of water constantly split and rejoined across the valley floor. Pregnant Angus cattle lifted their heads as we passed. Light broke through clouds in angled beams, catching mountain ridgelines in the distance.

Ben pulled out binoculars—he'd spotted a **Himalayan Tahr** on the peak.

At a disused green farm hut behind glacial melt, Ben dunked metal mugs directly into the flowing water. "The water's pure," he said, handing me one. "The best you'll taste in New Zealand." It was bone-chillingly cold and sharp, tasting like the mountains themselves.

We stood by the stream watching afternoon light dance across white-dusted peaks. The silver light on riverbeds turned heavy and metallic grey. Our window was closing.

## The Race Against the Storm

"We should turn around before the rain comes," Ben said.

As the car raced back across the basin, the sky split open with massive crepuscular rays—those "god rays" that burst downward from gaps in the clouds. They illuminated the turquoise waters of **Lake Tekapo** in the distance. The high-country light cycled through brilliant gold, then bruised purple. Then the sky sealed shut completely.

We'd found no black stilts on this particular run. But we'd found something equally valuable: access to one of Earth's most exclusive conservation dramas, where fewer than 200 birds stand between a species and oblivion.

## How to Join the Hunt

International flights run to **Christchurch** or **Queenstown**. From either airport, **Lake Tekapo** is approximately a three-hour drive.

**Tekapo Adventures** runs backcountry tours through private high-country stations in the Mackenzie Basin year-round from Tekapo township. The **Black Stilt Birding Tour** starts at **$399 NZD (£176)** per person for a three-hour experience. Spring (September through November) offers the highest probability of sightings during peak breeding season.

For more details on New Zealand birdwatching experiences, consult the [Department of Conservation's official guides](https://www.doc.govt.nz/) on native species and protected areas throughout the South Island.
