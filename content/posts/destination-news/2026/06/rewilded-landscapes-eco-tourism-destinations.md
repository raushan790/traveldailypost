I've witnessed something extraordinary unfolding across continents: landscapes once written off as ecological dead zones are erupting back to life. And the most surprising part? Tourism isn't destroying these restored ecosystems—it's saving them.

## The Rewilding Revolution Is Real

**Esteban Brenes-Mora**, a conservation biologist at **Re:wild**, still marvels at nature's resilience. "Nature's ability to bounce back never stops amazing me," he says, his voice tinged with genuine awe. He's not exaggerating. A decade ago, where **Tapir Valley Nature Reserve** now thrives in **Costa Rica's Tenorio Volcano National Park**, there was nothing but barren cattle ranch land ravaged by decades of industrial grazing. Today, the long-nosed tapirs—revered across Central America—have returned en masse.

This isn't isolated nostalgia. It's a tidal wave.

**Scotland: The Big Picture**, a social enterprise-turned-charity founded in 2008, now works with dozens of landowners across Scotland to drive large-scale nature recovery. What started as a fringe concept two decades ago has exploded into a major global movement. **Rewilding Europe** coordinates 100 initiatives spanning 29 countries. **Leonardo DiCaprio** has thrown his considerable weight behind **Re:wild**. Major institutions and billionaires are buying degraded land specifically to let nature reclaim it.

Yet here's the misconception that infuriates conservationists: rewilding isn't just for mega-rich landowners and Hollywood elites.

## From Overgrazing to Wilderness: The Ballintean Transformation

**Peter Cairns**, a renowned photographer and founder of Scotland: The Big Picture, owns **Ballintean Mountain Lodge**, nestled in the Cairngorm massif of the eastern Highlands. In 1995, the property was an over-grazed equestrian centre—abused land, depleted soil, stripped of life. Three decades of intentional non-intervention transformed it into something almost unrecognizable.

Today, teeming grasslands. Restored woodlands. Scrubland teeming with species diversity. A species-rich floodplain wrapping around the **River Feshie**. The entire property is a living mosaic of recovery.

"Ecological regeneration works at all scales," insists **Lisa Chilton**, CEO of Scotland: The Big Picture. "There's a misconception that rewilding is only for billionaires who can buy tens of thousands of acres. But it's far more inclusive and accessible than that."

This is the critical truth no one talks about.

## How Rewilding Actually Works

The mechanics are surprisingly straightforward: **stop the harm first**. Cease the intensive farming. End the logging. Eliminate overgrazing. Sometimes this requires active land purchase; more often, shifting industrial patterns create the opportunity naturally.

The **Lusatian Lakeland project** in eastern Germany exemplifies this. Disused opencast lignite mines are being deliberately flooded, creating **Europe's largest artificial wetland landscape**. Barren industrial scars are becoming biodiversity havens.

Water is the secret ingredient. As Chilton notes: "It's like when you dig a pond in the garden—you'll immediately see life attracted to it." Rivers flowing from restored mountain ecosystems create cascading downstream benefits. Lakes act as a supercharger for rewilding, triggering rapid ecological recovery.

Removing dams reinstates natural water flow. Allowing land to be grazed naturally—rather than mechanically cut—permits ecosystems to self-sustain. Species reintroduction—wolves in **Yellowstone**, lynx in **Germany's Harz mountains**—can trigger trophic cascades, where changes at the food chain's apex ripple down to regenerate every ecological layer.

But this is where the public narrative goes sideways.

## The Wolf Obsession Overshadows the Real Story

"It's just wolves, isn't it?" laughs **Sir Charles Burrell** when discussing rewilding. He's heard this countless times.

**Burrell and his wife, Isabella Tree**, are the UK's highest-profile rewilding power couple. Tree's 2019 bestseller, *Wilding*, chronicled their transformation of **Knepp Estate**—a failing 3,500-acre West Sussex farm—into a pioneering rewilding project. Today, Knepp teems with life that most Britons have never seen: herds of cattle, pigs, deer, and ponies roaming freely. Dragonflies swarm the water bodies. Purple emperor butterflies and nightingales—some of Britain's rarest creatures—have reclaimed the landscape.

Not a single wolf in sight.

"Rewilding is a deeper, more philosophical way of thinking about our future and how we interact with nature," Burrell stresses. The fixation on predator reintroduction masks the true revolution: a complete philosophical shift in how humans relate to ecosystems.

## The Misconception That Destroys Rewilding Support

Here's another critical myth: rewilding means excluding humans.

The inverse is actually true. Nearly every successful rewilding project thrives because of strategic human involvement. Local communities aren't pushed out—they're incentivized to stay. Eco-conscious tourists aren't banned—they're actively welcomed and generate revenue that sustains the entire operation.

**Knepp Estate** generates exponentially more income as a restored ecosystem than it ever did as a struggling farm. Tens of thousands of visitors annually walk the footpaths, book overnight stays, and join themed wildlife safaris. The **Lusatian Lakeland** now attracts thousands of eco-tourists seeking breaks in landscapes unrecognizable from their industrial past. **Tapir Valley Nature Reserve** has become a hotbed for eco-tourism.

"As a cattle ranch, perhaps one family benefitted economically," explains Brenes-Mora. "Now that it's switched to eco-tourism, perhaps a dozen families benefit. Rewilding opens up opportunities."

## Six Rewilded Destinations Worth Visiting

### Iberian Highlands, Spain

The **Southern Iberian Chain** mountain range had lost both its biodiversity and its people. Harsh winters and labour-intensive farming drove depopulation across decades. Enter the **Iberian Highlands rewilding project**: 2.1 million acres of desolate beauty combining elevated plateaus, dramatic river canyons, and isolated ancient forests—laced together by hiking and biking trails. Reintroduced **Cinereous vultures** soar overhead. Elusive **Iberian lynx** prowl the outcrops. **Iberian ibex**, wild horses, and tauros populate the rejuvenated grasslands. Three-hour rewilding safari: €25 (£22).

### Rutland Water, England

Celebrating its 50th anniversary in 2026, **Rutland Water** stands as one of **Europe's largest artificial lakes**. When hamlets were deliberately flooded to create this biodiversity hotspot five decades ago, nature responded explosively. A 1,000-acre reserve anchors the western end. Wildfowl and migratory birdlife sanctuary across the lake and wetlands. Water voles and ospreys anchor the reintroduced species list. The **Rutland Osprey Project** released its 300th bird last year—a genuine success story. A 23-mile perimeter path attracts walkers and cyclists. Ambitious plans for a 1,000-acre conservation attraction at the Burley Estate could soon introduce brown bears, wolves, and lynx. 90-minute osprey cruise: £31.

### Apoyo Lagoon, Nicaragua

This 23,000-year-old volcanic crater lake embodies conservation in a nation ravaged by deforestation. Its restoration catalyzes biodiversity recovery across the entire region, making it a critical emblem of Central American ecological resilience.

### Tenorio Volcano National Park, Costa Rica

Home to the cascading **Río Celeste waterfall** plunging 30 metres, this park represents the cutting edge of Central American rewilding. **Tapir Valley Nature Reserve** within its boundaries has become a destination for serious eco-tourists tracking the return of megafauna to restored habitats.

### Cairngorms, Scotland

The **Cairngorm massif** in Scotland's eastern Highlands has become emblematic of the nation's rewilding journey. Properties like **Ballintean Mountain Lodge** showcase how decades of intentional ecological stewardship can restore landscapes to their former richness—attracting travelers seeking authentic wilderness experiences.

### Lusatian Lakeland, Germany

**Europe's largest artificial wetland landscape** born from flooded lignite mines represents the ultimate redemption narrative. Former industrial scars are now thriving ecosystems attracting eco-tourists seeking the extraordinary story of ecological resurrection.

## The Economics of Restoration

Here's what government agencies and conservation boards are finally understanding: rewilding creates sustainable tourism revenue that outlasts extractive industries by decades.

A failing farm generates a single income stream. A rewilded landscape generates multiple revenue flows—guided eco-tours, overnight lodges, themed safaris, research permits, conservation volunteering programs. Communities don't merely survive; they thrive economically while stewarding ecosystems that will generate revenue for generations.

This is why [Rewilding Europe](https://rewildingeurope.com/) has attracted backing from institutional donors and why [Re:wild](https://www.rewild.org/) counts A-list celebrities among its supporters. The business case is airtight: ecological regeneration plus strategic tourism equals sustainable rural development.

The visitors aren't a burden—they're the engine that makes rewilding economically viable for landowners who might otherwise be tempted to exploit degraded land for short-term profits.

## What This Means for Travelers Right Now

If you've been seeking authentic nature experiences beyond sterile national parks and zoo-like reserves, rewilded landscapes represent the frontier. These aren't pristine wilderness areas unchanged for millennia—they're actively recovering ecosystems where humans and nature have negotiated a new relationship.

You're not just visiting these destinations. You're funding their survival.

*Nature doesn't need permission to return—it just needs permission to try.*
