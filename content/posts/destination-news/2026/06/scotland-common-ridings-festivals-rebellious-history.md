I was six years old, gripping the mane of **Frosty Friday**, a pure-white Shetland pony, galloping across my family's Ontario farm. I thought I was patrolling a kingdom. Decades later, after hanging up my spurs and circling back to the saddle across Iceland's volcanos and Argentina's Andes, I discovered something profound: my love of riding wasn't just passion—it was ancestral DNA.

My family traces back to **Scotland's Reivers**, those infamous bands of night-riding border raiders. That revelation pulled me north, toward the rolling hills of the **Scottish Borders**, where every summer more than a dozen towns explode into centuries-old tradition. These are the **Common Ridings**—among the world's largest and oldest equestrian spectacles—and their origins are far darker than the celebratory festivals they've become today.

## A Chaotic History Carved into the Borderlands

The **Common Ridings** didn't spring from pageantry. They emerged from blood, invasion, and survival.

In the late 13th century, **Edward I of England** launched his brutal campaign to claim Scotland, sparking the **Wars of Scottish Independence**. For three centuries afterward, border communities did what they had to do: they rode their boundaries. They patrolled. They defended.

But the most violent chapter came on **September 1513**, during the **Battle of Flodden**. King Henry VIII's forces massacred thousands of Scots in a single day—the deadliest clash the Borders would ever witness. Gordon Keddie, former Braw Lad and past president of Galashiels' Common Riding committee, explains the regional complexity: *"There's an old saying: 'they're the same, but they're different.' We've all got the same history behind us, but every town celebrates a different part."*

In Galashiels, they commemorate the **Battle of the Raid Stane** (1337), when local men defeated English invaders near a wild sour plum tree—and that tree became the town's badge. In Hawick, thousands gather to sing "Teribus," the war cry of Flodden's survivors, set to haunting bagpipes.

## The Reivers: Independence Twisted Into Lawlessness

Here's where my family's story gets complicated.

During this same era, riders called **Reivers** galloped across the Anglo-Scottish border under cover of darkness, mounted on hardy **Hobbler ponies**. They started as defenders. They morphed into something else entirely: murderers, cattle-thieves, and marauders driven only by clan loyalty.

The **Armstrong**, **Bell**, **Kerr**, and **Beatty** clans—my own ancestors—became synonymous with Reiver chaos. For 100 years leading up to **1603**, when **James VI of Scotland** ascended the English throne as James I, the borderlands descended into pure lawlessness. The new king was ruthless. He conspired with fellow Scots, hunted down the worst offending clans, and exiled them.

My Beatty ancestors were shipped to **Ulster (Northern Ireland)** in **1609** as part of the "Plantation of Ulster"—England's organized colonization of the Irish province. From raider to exile in generations.

## From Chaos to Celebration: The Victorian Revival

The Common Ridings vanished with the Reivers—but not permanently.

In **Victorian times**, border towns began commemorating their survival through annual festivals. By the early 20th century, what started as modest gatherings had swelled into week-long spectacles. Today, approximately **15 towns** across the Scottish Borders host these festivals, each with distinct ceremonies, elected leaders, and traditions. The season runs from late spring through **August**.

## Where History Still Breathes

The Scottish Borders aren't just festivals. They're a living museum of rebellion and resilience.

Just outside **Branxton village**, the **Flodden 1513 Ecomuseum** sits beside the actual battlefield where 10,000+ Scots fell. The **60-mile Border Reiver Trail** winds through rugged landscapes past **Gilnockie Tower** (Armstrong clan stronghold) and **Langholm Castle**. **Smailholm Tower**, perched dramatically on a windswept hilltop, and the foreboding **Hermitage Castle** (five miles north of Newcastleton) radiate this embattled history.

For ancestry travelers, the **Scottish Borders Archives** and **Registration Service** in Hawick hold genealogical treasures. **Abbotsford**, the grand 19th-century home of Sir Walter Scott (a Reiver descendant himself), located two miles east of Galashiels, offers literary context to this violent era.

## How to Saddle Up and Join the Ride

Reddit: *"Better felt than telt—it's better to experience these rides firsthand than hear about them."* — r/Scotland

That's the motto of **Cathy Joyce**, owner and expert guide of **Eventful Riders**, who matches horses to riders with surgical precision. (Her philosophy: *"Some are more whoa than go."*)

For experienced equestrians, joining a Common Riding is the most immersive way to grasp this history. You're not watching—you're galloping through it. Herd instinct kicks in when hundreds of horses thunder across open rolling countryside. Nerves can fray on the faster, more raucous ride-outs (Hawick earns a reputation for intensity).

**Lauder** offers the gentlest entry point. This town of 2,000, located 27 miles southeast of Edinburgh, hosts its Common Riding on the **first Saturday in August**, after a week of duck races, casual rides, and brass-band parades. It's among the oldest, smallest, and most social of all festivals. And yes—it's where my Beatty clan likely rode centuries ago.

## Planning Your Visit to the Scottish Borders

The Common Ridings season runs **late May through August**. Major festivals cluster around **June and July**, though Lauder closes out the summer in early **August**. For detailed dates and registration for specific ride-outs, consult [Eventful Riders](https://eventfulriders.co.uk) or individual town tourism boards.

The region is easily reached from **Edinburgh** (30-45 minutes by car to most towns). Accommodation ranges from rural farmstays to historic inns. The region's [tourism website](https://www.scot-borders.co.uk) provides comprehensive lodging and dining options.

If you're joining a ride, arrive 2-3 days early to acclimate to the horse and the terrain. Wear sturdy boots, a helmet, and layers—Scottish Borders weather shifts rapidly.
